# Settings and mounts

The **head** (also called the setting or mount) is the part that holds the centre stone. Pick it on the **Head** tab. This choice decides how much light reaches the stone, how safely it is held and how much cleaning the ring will want.

![Head tab]({{base}}screenshots/pages/builder.png)

In the builder each head is a card. A card is greyed out, with a reason, when the head was not designed for your current cut. Heads also show three small rating dots from the component data: **Protection**, **Brilliance** and **Upkeep** (more dots on upkeep means easier to clean). Open any head on the [Components](#/components/head) pages for its full notes.

## The three trade-offs

| You want                           | You give up                                              |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Maximum sparkle (open, airy heads) | Some protection, since fewer metal parts guard the edges |
| Maximum protection (bezels, flush) | Some light entering the sides, and a different look      |
| Easy cleaning (simple heads)       | Extra stones and detail that make a ring special         |

No head wins on all three. The summary tables below help you choose.

## Prong family

Prongs are small metal claws that hold the stone. More prongs means more security. Fewer prongs shows more of the stone.

| Head              | Security        | Brilliance | Upkeep | Notes                                                 |
| ----------------- | --------------- | ---------- | ------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Solitaire 4-prong | Good            | High       | Easy   | Classic. Shows the stone and lets light in.           |
| Solitaire 6-prong | Very good       | High       | Easy   | Extra security. A round stone looks rounder.          |
| 3-prong           | Fair            | Very high  | Easy   | Minimal metal. Modern. Best for round or trillion.    |
| Double-claw       | Very good       | High       | Medium | Pairs of prongs at each position, with a bolder look. |
| Split prong       | Good            | High       | Medium | Each prong splits into two tines at the top.          |
| V-prong           | Good for points | High       | Easy   | A V-shaped prong that wraps a sharp corner.           |

### Solitaire 4-prong and 6-prong

![Solitaire 4-prong]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/solitaire-4-prong.png)
![Solitaire 6-prong]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/solitaire-6-prong.png)

A four-prong head shows the most stone and makes a round look square-ish between the prongs. Six prongs add safety margin: if one prong is damaged, the stone is still held. It is also the traditional look for an older style of diamond solitaire. Either suits round, oval and cushion cuts. For princess stones, a four-prong with the prongs on the corners is natural. For both, have prongs checked once a year.

### 3-prong and double-claw

![3-prong]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/3-prong.png)
![Double-claw]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/double-claw.png)

Three prongs are the lightest option. They suit round and trillion shapes well. They give less margin for error than four, so they are better for a protected life. Double-claw heads use paired prongs at each corner for extra grip on cushion, radiant and emerald shapes.

### Split prong and V-prong

![Split prong]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/split-prong.png)
![V-prong]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/v-prong.png)

V-prongs are the answer for any shape with a point: pear, marquise, heart, trillion, kite. The V covers the exposed tip. Split prongs add a fine, vintage-feeling detail at the top and were common in mid-century designs.

## Basket, cathedral and trellis

| Head           | Security | Brilliance | Upkeep                               |
| -------------- | -------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Basket         | Good     | High       | Medium (dirt gathers in the lattice) |
| Cathedral head | Good     | High       | Medium                               |
| Trellis        | Good     | High       | Medium                               |

![Basket]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/basket.png)
![Cathedral head]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/cathedral-head.png)
![Trellis]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/trellis.png)

A **basket** holds the stone in a woven gallery of metal under the girdle. It is a very traditional shape that works with nearly every cut. **Cathedral head** uses arches that rise to meet the stone, framing and lifting it (a cathedral shank carries the same idea down the band, see [Shanks and styles](#/docs/shanks-and-styles)). **Trellis** prongs cross each other like a lattice for a delicate, more ornamental look.

## Bezel family

A bezel wraps the stone's edge (girdle) in a thin metal rim.

| Head          | Security  | Brilliance     | Upkeep    |
| ------------- | --------- | -------------- | --------- |
| Full bezel    | Highest   | Medium         | Very easy |
| Half bezel    | Very high | Medium to high | Easy      |
| Flush (gypsy) | Highest   | Medium         | Very easy |

![Full bezel]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/bezel-full.png)
![Half bezel]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/bezel-half.png)
![Flush gypsy]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/flush-gypsy.png)

A full bezel gives the best protection of any head, and a smooth, snag-free profile. That makes it ideal for active hands, for nurses and chefs, and for soft stones like opal or emerald. The trade-off is that metal covers part of the stone. A half bezel leaves the two ends open (or the sides, depending on direction) for a little more light. A flush or gypsy setting sinks the stone into the metal with the table level, giving a modern, sleek line.

The **polygon bezel** and **cushion bezel** keep the burnished rim against the girdle but give the outside a hexagon, octagon or cushion outline, with an optional stepped second tier and open posts or a solid skirt below. They suit Art Deco, Victorian and modern geometric looks.

Bezels are one of the trends cited in 2026 industry coverage. See [Gallery and examples](#/docs/gallery-and-examples) for where the sources live.

## Halo family

A halo surrounds the centre stone with small accent stones. It makes the centre look bigger, adds sparkle and offers a ring of protection.

| Head                        | Security | Brilliance | Upkeep                              |
| --------------------------- | -------- | ---------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Halo (round, cushion, oval) | Good     | Very high  | Harder (more small stones to clean) |
| Hidden halo                 | Good     | High       | Medium                              |
| Double halo                 | Good     | Very high  | Hardest                             |

![Halo round]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/halo-round.png)
![Halo cushion]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/halo-cushion.png)
![Halo oval]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/halo-oval.png)
![Hidden halo]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/hidden-halo.png)
![Double halo]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/double-halo.png)

The **hexagon halo** sets the stones along an elongated hexagon that stretches to the centre stone. Choose the halo outline that echoes your centre stone: round halo for round, cushion halo for cushion or princess, oval halo for oval or pear. The accent stones' size, material and colour are set on the **Accents** tab. A **hidden halo** puts the accent stones below the centre stone, visible from the side but not from above, so the top view stays a clean solitaire. A **double halo** repeats the ring of stones for maximum sparkle and size, and it is the most elaborate to clean and repair.

## Multi-stone heads

| Head        | Extra stones        | Notes                                                     |
| ----------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Three-stone | 2 side stones       | Past, present and future. A stable, balanced look.        |
| Five-stone  | 4 side stones       | A graduated row. Often used for a larger statement.       |
| Cluster     | Many small stones   | Several stones arranged to read as one bigger stone.      |
| Toi et moi  | A second main stone | "You and me": two stones side by side.                    |
| Bypass head | Optional            | Two arms pass each other, holding the stone between them. |

![Three-stone]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/three-stone.png)
![Five-stone]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/five-stone.png)
![Cluster]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/cluster.png)
![Toi et moi]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/toi-et-moi.png)
![Bypass head]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/bypass-head.png)

The **cluster flank** head sets one to five small tilted stones on each side of the centre, in a column, a chevron or a graduated row.

Side stones (round, baguette, trapezoid, pear) are set on the Accents tab. The retailer data we gathered specifies side stones by total weight, because buyers shop that way. Toi et moi works well with a pair of pear or oval stones, or two contrasting gems, and lab-grown pairs make it affordable. A bypass head often sits well with an east-west stone.

## Special heads

![Tension]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/tension.png)
![Compass]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/compass.png)
![Vintage filigree]({{base}}screenshots/components/head/vintage-filigree.png)

- **Tension.** The stone appears to float, held by pressure from the band ends. It is striking and minimal, but needs precise manufacturing, tends to be done in platinum or hard gold, and is hard to resize. Smaller, harder stones suit it best, and it is not the right choice for soft gems.
- **Compass.** Metal details sit at the compass points (north, south, east and west) of the stone. See the component page for exactly how the app models it.
- **Tension bypass.** Tension jaws swept across the finger so the two blades pass each other like a bypass band. It pairs with a bypass or spiral shank and has the same sizing limits as a plain tension setting.
- **Art Deco plaque.** A domed octagonal plaque of pierced ribs, milgrain and rim diamonds round an old-cut stone.
- **Vintage filigree.** Openwork, pierced and engraved metal lace beneath and around the stone. Art Deco and Edwardian inspired, often with milgrain edges. Lovely, but dirt collects in the openwork, so it wants a soft brush.

## Which head for which cut?

| Cut                           | Strong choices                                                        |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Round brilliant               | Almost anything. 4-prong or 6-prong for maximum light; halo for size. |
| Oval                          | 4-prong, hidden halo, halo oval, bezel.                               |
| Cushion                       | Double-claw, halo cushion, bezel, basket.                             |
| Princess                      | 4-prong on the corners, V-prong, bezel, halo.                         |
| Emerald cut, Asscher, radiant | Corner prongs or double-claw, bezel, three-stone with baguettes.      |
| Pear, marquise, heart         | V-prong or bezel at the point. Three-stone works well.                |
| Trillion                      | V-prong, bezel, side stones.                                          |
| Cabochon, rose cut            | Bezel, which protects the edge.                                       |

## Choosing for your life

- **Active or manual work:** full bezel, flush, or a six-prong with low profile (reduce the Raise slider).
- **Maximum sparkle:** 3-prong or 4-prong, cathedral, with a low gallery.
- **A bigger look on a budget:** halo, hidden halo or cluster.
- **Soft gems:** bezel or halo, never a tension.
- **Vintage:** filigree, trellis or basket, with milgrain, in platinum or rose gold.

Raise, tilt, rotate and offset (the placement controls) can change how any head sits. See [Building a ring](#/docs/building-a-ring).

Pair your head with the right shank in [Shanks and styles](#/docs/shanks-and-styles).
