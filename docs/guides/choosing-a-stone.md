# Choosing a stone

The centre stone sets the look, most of the cost and most of the care a ring needs. In the builder it lives on the **Stone** tab: material, colour, cut, carat, clarity and (for diamond-like stones) colour grade.

![Stone tab in the builder]({{base}}screenshots/pages/builder.png)

## Start with how it will be worn

Ask one question first: will this ring be worn every day, including at the gym, in the garden and while washing up? If yes, durability should outrank everything else. A hard, tough stone in a protective setting will look good for decades. A soft stone can still be gorgeous, but it asks for habits (taking the ring off, a bezel, regular checks).

## Material at a glance

Hardness here is the Mohs number stored with each gem in the app. Mohs measures scratch resistance only. It says nothing about toughness, meaning how well a stone survives a knock, and some hard stones still chip or split along cleavage planes.

| Gem | Mohs | Everyday-ring verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diamond (natural or lab-grown) | 10 | Excellent. The scratch-resistance benchmark. |
| Moissanite | 9.25 | Excellent. Very hard and tough. |
| Sapphire, ruby | 9 | Excellent. Sapphire and ruby are the same mineral, corundum, in different colours. |
| Alexandrite | 8.5 | Very good, and rare. |
| Spinel | 8 | Very good. A durable, often overlooked alternative. |
| Topaz | 8 | Good scratch resistance, but it has a cleavage plane, so protect it from sharp knocks. |
| Emerald, aquamarine, morganite | 7.5 | Good with care. Emerald in particular is often full of internal fractures and benefits from a bezel or a gentle lifestyle. |
| Tourmaline | 7.25 | Fair to good with care. |
| Garnet, amethyst, citrine, zircon | 7 | Acceptable with care. Dust contains quartz at about 7, so these can gradually dull. |
| Peridot | 6.75 | Better for occasional wear or a protective setting. |
| Onyx | 6.75 | Mostly used as accents or in bold bezels. |
| Tanzanite | 6.5 | Soft and cleaves. Choose a protective setting, remove it for rough work. |
| Opal | 5.5 | Delicate. Usually a cabochon in a bezel, worn with care. |
| Pearl | 3 | Very soft and sensitive to chemicals. Better for occasional wear. |

A rough rule: 8 and above is comfortable for daily wear, 7 to 7.5 is fine with sensible care, and below 7 is a ring you take off for chores.

## The 4 Cs

The 4 Cs are the standard language for diamonds. The app uses the same grading vocabulary so you can follow what a jeweller or certificate says.

### Cut

Cut here means the quality of the proportions and polish, which controls sparkle. It is different from the shape (round, oval, and so on), which the app calls the cut too. For shape, see [Stone cuts](#/docs/stone-cuts). For round diamonds, labs grade quality from Excellent down. For fancy shapes, there is usually no official grade, so look at the stone itself: even sparkle across the face, no dark or washed-out areas.

### Colour

For colourless diamonds the scale runs from D (no colour) to Z (light yellow or brown). The app groups it into five bands:

![Colorless D-F]({{base}}screenshots/components/color-grade/d-f.png)

| Band | Letters | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colorless | D-F | No detectable colour. The most expensive. |
| Near-colorless | G-J | Looks white face-up to most people, especially once set. Often the best value. |
| Faint | K-M | A hint of warmth, easy to see in a platinum or white-gold setting. |
| Very light | N-R | Visible yellow tint. |
| Light | S-Z | Clearly tinted. |

Yellow and rose gold hide a little warmth, so G-J stones look especially good in them. A platinum or white-gold setting reveals more.

### Clarity

Clarity describes internal marks (inclusions) and surface marks (blemishes) seen at 10x magnification. The app offers FL, IF, VVS1, VVS2, VS1, VS2, SI1, SI2, I1 and I2.

| Grade | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| FL, IF | Nothing visible at 10x (IF may have tiny surface marks). Premium price for no visible benefit. |
| VVS1, VVS2 | Marks extremely hard to find even under magnification. |
| VS1, VS2 | Minor marks. Clean to the naked eye in nearly all stones. A popular sweet spot. |
| SI1, SI2 | Marks may be visible without magnification. Inspect each stone. |
| I1, I2 | Marks obvious and sometimes affecting brilliance or strength. |

The 3D preview shows a gentle haze for lower grades so you can judge the trend, but it is not a substitute for looking at a real stone.

![VS1 clarity]({{base}}screenshots/components/clarity/vs1.png)

### Carat

Carat is weight, not size. One carat is 0.2 grams. Two stones of the same weight can look different because of their shape, depth and the material's density.

## Carat to millimetres

The builder shows millimetres next to the carat slider (0.25 to 5 ct). It uses the stone's density and typical proportions, so the number changes with material and cut.

The table below is a guide for **diamonds**. Round values are the commonly quoted figures. The other shapes are estimates scaled from a typical one-carat stone, using the fact that weight grows with the cube of size. Real stones vary by a few tenths of a millimetre depending on proportions.

| Carat | Round (diameter) | Oval (L x W) | Emerald (L x W) | Cushion (side) | Pear (L x W) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.50 | 5.1 mm | 6.1 x 4.5 mm | 5.4 x 3.8 mm | 4.5 mm | 6.4 x 4.2 mm |
| 0.75 | 5.8 mm | 7.0 x 5.2 mm | 6.2 x 4.4 mm | 5.2 mm | 7.3 x 4.8 mm |
| 1.00 | 6.5 mm | 7.7 x 5.7 mm | 6.8 x 4.8 mm | 5.7 mm | 8.0 x 5.3 mm |
| 1.50 | 7.4 mm | 8.8 x 6.5 mm | 7.8 x 5.5 mm | 6.5 mm | 9.2 x 6.1 mm |
| 2.00 | 8.2 mm | 9.7 x 7.2 mm | 8.6 x 6.0 mm | 7.2 mm | 10.1 x 6.7 mm |
| 3.00 | 9.3 mm | 11.1 x 8.2 mm | 9.8 x 6.9 mm | 8.2 mm | 11.5 x 7.6 mm |

Two practical takeaways. First, elongated shapes (oval, pear, emerald) show more surface for their weight than a deep round does, which is why an oval often looks larger than a round of the same carat. Second, doubling the weight does not double the width: it adds only about 26% to the diameter.

### Other materials

Because the app uses density, the same carat gives a different size:

| Material | Density (g/cm3) | One carat compared with a diamond |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diamond, lab-grown diamond | 3.52 | Baseline |
| Moissanite | 3.22 | About 3% larger across |
| Sapphire, ruby | 4.0 | About 4% smaller across |
| Emerald | 2.72 | About 9% larger across |

So when you swap materials in the builder at a fixed carat, expect the stone to change size slightly. That is correct, not a bug.

## Lab-grown versus natural

Lab-grown diamonds are real diamonds: the same crystal structure, hardness (10), refractive index and sparkle. They are grown rather than mined, and they sell for a fraction of the natural price. In the app, the "Lab-grown and synthetic" group in the material list holds them. The price hints stored with the gems reflect that: roughly 500 to 1,500 US dollars per carat for lab-grown diamond and 3,500 to 12,000 for natural near one carat, both informational and moving fast.

Things to weigh:

- **Value.** A lab-grown stone gets you a bigger or higher-grade stone for the same budget.
- **Resale and rarity.** Natural stones are finite and tend to hold resale value better. Lab-grown prices have been falling.
- **Origin.** Some people prefer a stone with a mined story, others prefer a lab origin. Neither looks different to the eye.
- **Disclosure.** Reputable sellers state origin and may laser-inscribe the girdle. Ask for a grading report.

## Moissanite

Moissanite is a lab-made silicon carbide gem. It is hard (9.25), tough and affordable, with more "fire" (rainbow flashes, dispersion about 0.104 against diamond's 0.044) and a higher refractive index (2.65 against 2.417). Many people love the lively sparkle. Some find it a little rainbow-heavy compared with a diamond's cleaner white flashes. It is also a great budget match for a white-metal ring.

## Coloured gems

Coloured stones are having a long moment, and they offer personality at lower cost than a large diamond. A few tips:

- **Sapphire** comes in every colour, not only blue. It is the best all-round coloured choice for daily wear.
- **Ruby** shares sapphire's hardness. Fine colour is expensive.
- **Emerald** is classic green but brittle in practice. Bezels and halos protect the edges. Many emeralds are treated with oils or resin, which is normal but worth asking about.
- **Aquamarine and morganite** are soft pastel beryls that look larger for their price, and sit well in rose or white gold.
- **Alexandrite** shifts colour between daylight and incandescent light. Rare and costly.
- **Spinel** is hard, vivid and good value.
- **Opal, pearl, tanzanite, peridot** are best as occasional rings or in sheltered bezels.

Choose the colour swatch on the Stone tab to preview shades. For heads that protect soft gems, see [Settings and mounts](#/docs/settings-and-mounts).

## Treatments and certificates

Many coloured stones are heated or treated to improve colour, and that is normal. What matters is disclosure. For significant stones, ask for a report from an independent laboratory that states the identity, origin (natural or lab-grown), treatments and, for diamonds, the 4 Cs.

## A sensible way to decide

1. Pick the material for your lifestyle (use the Mohs table).
2. Pick the shape you love ([Stone cuts](#/docs/stone-cuts)).
3. Set a size in millimetres you like on the finger, not just a carat number.
4. Spend where the eye sees it: cut quality first, then colour, then clarity. Settle on an eye-clean grade such as VS2 or SI1 if you inspect it, and a near-colourless G-J if the metal is warm.
5. Match the metal and head to the stone ([Metals](#/docs/metals-and-mixed-metals)).
