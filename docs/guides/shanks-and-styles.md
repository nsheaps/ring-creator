# Shanks and styles

The **shank** is the part of the ring that wraps your finger. It sets comfort, how the ring feels day to day, and a lot of the style. Choose it on the **Shank** tab. Each shank card shows two rating dots, **Comfort** and **Durability**, and a "Set with stones" note when the shank carries its own gems.

![Shank tab]({{base}}screenshots/pages/builder.png)

## Shank cross-section basics

Look at a shank end-on and you see its profile. These are the shapes behind most names:

- **Round:** a half-round or full-round wire. Classic.
- **Flat:** a flat inside and outside. Modern, with a bolder presence.
- **Comfort fit:** domed on the inside so the ring slides on easily.
- **Knife-edge:** a ridge on the outside, a bit like a blade seen from the end.

The width you choose (see the options under each shank card) matters as much as the name. Retailer listings we collected show slender shanks of about 1.4 to 2.5 mm for most solitaires and halos, with wider shanks of 3 mm and up used for statement styles. Thicker "cigar band" shanks have been a recent trend.

| Shank | Look | Comfort | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plain round | A smooth rounded band. The timeless default. | High | Strong, easy to resize, goes with anything. |
| Plain flat | Flat top and sides. Contemporary and architectural. | Medium to high | Pairs well with bezels and emerald cuts. |
| Knife edge | The outside narrows to a ridge. Crisp and elegant. | Medium | Makes a stone look larger by contrast. Thin at the ridge, so choose a sturdier metal. |
| Comfort fit | Domed inside for easy sliding. | Highest | Slightly more metal, more durable. |

### Tapered and cathedral

![Tapered]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/tapered.png)
![Cathedral]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/cathedral.png)

A **tapered** shank is wider at the shoulders near the stone and slims toward the bottom, which shows off the head and keeps the underside comfortable. A **cathedral** shank arches up on either side to cradle the head, like the vaults of a church, lifting the stone and adding a classic profile. Cathedral shanks look good under a basket head.

### Split and crossover shanks

![Split]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/split.png)
![Crossover]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/crossover.png)
![Bypass]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/bypass.png)

A **split** shank divides into two strands near the head, widening the visual base and often carrying pave stones. **Crossover** strands cross like an X below the stone. **Bypass** strands pass each other and curl to either side of the stone instead of meeting. Bypass shanks pair with kite, floral or east-west heads. The offset slider (Head tab) can slide the stone along the band to make the asymmetry more pronounced.

### Stone-set shanks

![Pave]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/pave.png)
![Channel]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/channel.png)

**Pave** shanks are paved with tiny stones set closely, almost no metal visible between them. They add sparkle from the side and bring the glitter around the finger. **Channel** shanks sit stones in a groove between two metal walls, producing a smooth, snag-resistant surface that is tougher than pave. Both are set on the Accents tab: choose the accent stone material, cut and size. Resizing a fully stone-set shank is limited, so check with your jeweller.

### Decorative shanks

![Twisted]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/twisted.png)
![Braided]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/braided.png)
![Hammered]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/hammered.png)
![Milgrain]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/milgrain.png)
![Vintage filigree]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/vintage-filigree.png)

- **Twisted:** two strands winding around each other. Romantic and tactile.
- **Braided:** three or more strands woven together, with a rope-like look.
- **Hammered:** a surface of tiny dimples. Hand-made and a little rustic. It hides scratches well.
- **Milgrain:** tiny beads along the edges, an Edwardian and Art Deco hallmark.
- **Vintage filigree:** pierced, openwork metal lace. Pretty, but wants a soft brush to keep clean.

### Nature shanks

![Leaf and vine]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/leaf-vine.png)
![Twig]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/twig.png)

**Leaf and vine** and **twig** shanks use organic curves, usually in rose or yellow gold, and are often paired with morganite, moissanite or a pale coloured stone. They are a signature of nature-inspired and cottagecore rings.

### Slim and wide

![Dainty]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/dainty.png)
![Wide]({{base}}screenshots/components/shank/wide.png)

**Dainty** is a fine, delicate band that lets the stone dominate. It looks light but is easier to bend, so match it with a strong metal such as platinum or 14k gold. **Wide** is a chunky band for a bold, modern look. Wide bands can feel snug, so many people size up slightly (see [Wedding bands and fit](#/docs/wedding-bands-and-fit)).

## Style families

Many rings fall into recognisable families. These are not rules, only starting points. Each lists typical ingredients you can choose in the builder.

| Family | Typical head | Typical shank | Typical metal | Typical stone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solitaire | Solitaire 4-prong, 6-prong, bezel | Plain round, knife edge, tapered, dainty | Platinum, white or yellow gold | Round, oval, emerald cut |
| Halo | Halo round, halo cushion, halo oval | Pave, split, tapered | Platinum, white gold | Round, oval, cushion |
| Three-stone | Three-stone | Plain round, tapered, channel | Platinum, white or yellow gold | Round or oval centre, baguette or pear sides |
| Vintage | Vintage filigree, basket, trellis | Vintage filigree, milgrain, cathedral | Platinum, rose gold | Old European, old mine, cushion |
| Modern | Bezel, flush, tension, hidden halo | Plain flat, wide, knife edge | Yellow gold, platinum | Emerald cut, oval, kite, hexagon |
| Nature | 4-prong, leaf-style heads | Leaf and vine, twig, hammered | Rose gold, yellow gold | Morganite, moissanite, round |
| Art Deco | Vintage filigree, split prong, cathedral | Milgrain, vintage filigree | Platinum | Asscher, emerald cut, old European |

### Solitaire

One stone, nothing competing with it. Highly versatile and easy to wear with a stack of bands. A solitaire is very sensitive to proportions, so use the placement controls to find the right height.

### Halo

A ring of accent stones frames the centre, so the whole thing looks larger and sparklier. Mix a halo with a pave or split shank for a glamorous look. A hidden halo is a quieter variant.

### Three-stone

A centre flanked by two stones, often read as past, present and future. Side stones can be round, baguette, trapezoid or pear. Retail listings show side stones specified by their combined weight, typically a fraction of the centre stone.

### Vintage

Look for milgrain, filigree, rose gold, platinum and older cuts with a soft glow. Edwardian and Art Deco styles favour platinum, geometry and openwork. Victorian styles lean toward rose gold, clusters and bezels.

### Modern

Clean lines, flat shanks, bezels and flush settings. Elongated east-west stones feel current. Yellow gold has been rising in popularity.

### Nature

Leaves, twigs and vines in warm metal. Pastel stones work well. Matte or hammered finishes complete the look.

### Art Deco

Geometric symmetry, step cuts, contrasting stones and precise edges. Think Asscher or emerald cut, milgrain, openwork and platinum or white metal.

## Mixing styles

Combine features with intent. These patterns usually work:

- **Plain head, decorated shank:** a simple solitaire head on a pave or twisted shank. The shank carries the interest.
- **Decorated head, plain shank:** a halo or filigree head on a plain round or dainty shank. The head carries the interest.
- **Keep one idea per ring.** If the head is ornate, keep the shank quiet.
- **Match the eras:** milgrain and filigree belong with vintage cuts; flat shanks and bezels with modern cuts.
- **Repeat a detail:** if the band has milgrain, add milgrain to the shank finish to tie them.

What clashes: a heavily paved shank under a heavily detailed head can overwhelm the stone. A very wide shank under a tiny stone can swallow it. A knife-edge shank under a bezel can look unbalanced.

You can see every option live in the builder; changing the shank keeps your head and metal. For the metal side, read [Metals and mixed metals](#/docs/metals-and-mixed-metals).
